PoliteChat

An AI tool that rewrites rude messages into professional ones

Interaction Design Critical Design Live Performance

The Problem

In daily life and work, we constantly need to write emails, reply to colleagues, message clients — all in a polite, professional tone. But writing that way doesn't come naturally to everyone, especially when you're communicating in a second language.

The Concept

PoliteChat is an AI chatbot that rewrites your message in a polite, professional tone. You type what you actually mean — even if it's blunt or frustrated — and it instantly gives you a version you can send. Think of it like Grammarly, but for tone. The vision is a browser plugin that works inside Gmail, Slack, or any chat app — softening your language right where you need it.

Why I built this

As a Chinese speaker, I've felt this gap firsthand. Chinese communication tends to be more direct — but in English professional settings, that directness often reads as rude. I built PoliteChat because I personally needed it. I trained the model on hand-curated sentence pairs, designed the full web UI in Figma, and built and tested the whole system end to end — entirely on my own.

Scope Solo project — concept, UI, model training, testing
Use cases Email drafting, workplace chat, customer service
Vision Browser plugin like Grammarly — works anywhere you type
Built with Figma (UI) · Custom dataset · Fine-tuned language model
Output Working web app + live performance piece

Spark

A mobile app that turns your big goals into small daily tasks

UI Design Mobile Figma

The Problem

Most people have goals — go to the gym, finish a side project, build a new habit — but struggle to follow through. Existing planning apps are either too complicated or designed for teams, not for individuals navigating the transition from student life to the working world.

The Concept

Spark is a mobile app designed for students and recent graduates who want to turn big goals into small, manageable daily actions. Set a goal — say, go to the gym for 30 days — and Spark breaks it into today's task, tracks your streak on a calendar, and shows your progress as a simple ring. No complexity, no overwhelm.

Design Decisions

I referenced Canvas — the platform almost every student already uses — as a baseline for familiar interaction patterns. Users who've used Canvas already know how to navigate a task list, check things off, and view a calendar. That familiarity made Spark feel instantly approachable. I chose circular progress rings over percentages because rings feel motivating rather than mathematical. Tasks are grouped by "Today" and "Tomorrow" — not by project — because that's how people actually think about their day.

This was a fully independent project: I defined the concept, designed every screen, built the interactive prototype, and ran usability testing myself.

Scope Solo project — research, UX, UI, prototyping, testing
Target users Students and recent graduates (18–26)
Key reference Canvas — familiar patterns, low learning curve
Design choices Progress rings, day-scoped tasks, calm visual tone
Built with Figma — clickable prototype ready to test

Artist Statement

I make interactive websites and take photographs. In my web projects, I start with things people already know how to use, like a chat box, a keyboard, or an online store. I’m interested in what happens when these familiar tools do something unexpected. In Chaos in Common, pressing a key makes a sound and sends particles across the screen. In Shadow Market, visitors browse a fictional store and decide which sellers they would trust.

Speaking Mandarin and English has made me notice how differently people can read the same message. A sentence that sounds direct to one person might sound rude to someone else. That experience led me to make PoliteChat, a tool that helps people rewrite messages for work while keeping what they want to say.

Photography has shaped how I design, too. When I take a portrait, I pay attention to what I include in the frame and where the viewer’s eye goes first. I think about the same things when I design a website. The placement of a button or the wording of a label can change what someone does next. I want people to explore my work at their own pace and see what happens when they interact with it.
